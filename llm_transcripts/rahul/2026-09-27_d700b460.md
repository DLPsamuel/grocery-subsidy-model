I've gone through the model and checked it against every data file in the repo. One thing up front: **nobody has written the model as code yet.** It only exists in the Level 1 v3 document. So today we're mostly checking which ingredients we have before we start cooking.

## What the model does

The model works like a recipe with four steps:

1. **How much each store appeals to people.** Every family in Hunts Point/Longwood gives each store a score. The score depends on how good the store is, how cheap it is, and how far away it is. Poorer families care more about price and distance.
2. **Where people shop.** Those scores turn into chances, for example "a low-income family picks Key Food 30% of the time."
3. **What the subsidy changes.** The city gives one store some money. The store passes part of it (about 65%) on to shoppers as lower prices. Lower prices raise that store's score.
4. **How much families gain.** The model turns the higher scores into a dollar amount of benefit for the whole neighborhood. Then it answers two questions:
   - **Goal 1:** What's the smallest subsidy that makes a real difference?
   - **Goal 2:** Which store is the best one to give the money to?

## The ingredients: what we have and what's missing

## Things that already look off

3. **Supermarkets might get almost no shoppers.** We flagged this earlier. With the doc's price-sensitivity numbers, the model can predict that almost nobody picks a supermarket. We'll see once we run it.
4. **The benefit covers one shopping trip, not a year.** The dollar gain in the doc is per trip. To get a yearly number we have to multiply by about 52 trips.

κ = **0.60**: about **60% of a family's grocery bill** goes on the items that get the 30% discount. So at a N.Y.C. Groceries store, the whole bill drops by about **0.30 × 0.60 = 18%**. That matches the "effective cut 18%" Chris already has in his plan, which is a good sign.

**How I got it**
1. **Which items get the discount:** the city's plan (RFP Appendix C) lists all produce, fresh meat and fish, plus staples like milk, eggs, cheese, bread, rice, pasta, beans, cereal and soup. Snacks and desserts are out.
2. **How families spend:** national government data (BLS, 2024) splits the average $6,224 yearly grocery bill into 18 categories.
3. **Sort the categories:**

| | Examples | Share of the bill |
|---|---|---|
| **Fully counts** | fresh fruit and veg, meat, fish, eggs, milk | 37% |
| **Partly counts** | bread (only sandwich bread), dairy (no ice cream), "misc foods" (soup yes, chips no) | 46% |
| **Doesn't count** | sweets, soda and juice | 17% |

The partly-counting categories are the tricky part, so I give three versions:
- **Low (0.37):** only what fully counts
- **High (0.83):** everything that partly counts, too
- **Base (0.60):** half of the partly-counting part
